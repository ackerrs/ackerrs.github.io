The presence of Artificial Intelligence (AI) and machine learning as tools is an awkward conundrum for the software community. On one hand, it provides capacity for complex automation, considerably easier information consolidation, and there are many truly fascinating fields of study and work to be discovered and explored. Conversely, AI is biting the other hand of the one who feeds it. From an education viewpoint, students are able to generate entire essays or get answers to homework without learning anything about the subject, writing in general, or critical thinking skills. From a professional perspective, many software engineers and other IT positions are being terminated in favor of "inexpensive" AI solutions. From a user and customer level, many companies implement AI "features" that don't meet the touted claims of capabilities and introduce deeply concerning vulnerabilities in systems for no more reason than they can.

## A Fossil with a Bone to Pick

Perhaps it shows my age, but the internet was a relatively new resource growing up. Essays required handwritten brainstorms, handwritten rough drafts, and maybe it was permitted to type out the final essay. Citing websites for information was scorned, and it took hours of digging through the library to find enough sources on a subject to pass muster. Today, half of my classmates in ENG100 were unable to formulate even a rough out of overarching concept, paragraph targets, and tying them all together.

Some of the tools available for coding present similar issues. A quick jaunt to Claude.ai and some "prompt engineering" results in functional code, yet the user has obtained zero capacity for understanding the information and likely cannot explain why the code is written the way it is. For collegiate assignments, perhaps that is adequate to get a passing grade, but in the professional world an inability to read, comprehend, and develop more complex programs is crippling. Even more mundane uses of CoPilot in Visual Studio Code can fix issues in a program, but if the user does not read the explanation for the changes they will continue to make the same mistakes. Using AI recklessly further stunts vital skills beyond writing code: if a robot does all the thinking, users won't develop critical thinking and problem solving skills that apply to the rest of their lives.

There is an additional issue stemming from corporate mismanagement and the Dunning-Kruger effect. AI is a hot topic, and upper echelons of management in companies the world over demand implementing it in products for no more reason than to print "AI" on the box. Poorly developed or unnecessary features can degrade customer experiences, such as children's toys that promote sex, violence, and hatespeech. Even properly integrated AI is often a solution in search of a problem: does a refrigerator truly need intelligence to produce a grocery list?

## Navigating a Slippery Slope

The siren's song that is AI is an issue because of how useful a tool it can be. It can consolidate information regarding a subject far faster and more concisely than a google search result will. Seeing examples of how a function can be called is greatly enhanced compared to the oft confusing technically written definitions in many user's manuals for programming languages. AI can even act as a tutor, being provided a sample of code and asking specific questions such as "why does this code return 3 when I provide 1 + 1" can reveal a user's lack of understanding on how a language functions.

Software Engineering (ICS 314) provides many opportunities to use AI. For Workout of the Days (WODs) and the final project, CoPilot and the google search AI were convenient tools for looking up syntax, correcting simple mistakes in the code, or explaining why the code does not work as written. It is probable many students used it to simply generate solutions, whether at home, practicing in class, or the actual quiz, but it is a great disservice to themselves in the long run. Essay writing is in a similar position: looking up information (and verifying its accuracy) saved hours of reading through many websites to find what was needed. It could even be a powerful tool in brainstorming or helping English as a Second Language (ESL) students with grammar. Far less interesting is using AI to generate the essay itself: they tend to be uninformative, vague, and genuinely worthless in terms of content.

For better or worse, Artificial Intelligence is here to stay. The multitudinous features are a rich collection of tools that can assist in building knowledge and products. Perhaps the solution to the downsides rests in humanity: in order to not think of every tool as a hammer, we must first realize that not every problem is a nail.
