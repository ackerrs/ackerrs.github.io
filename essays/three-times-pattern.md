Pareidolia: an evolutionary trait virtually all humans share that grants us the super power of seeing things that aren't really there. Seeing faces and shapes in objects that are not faces or (those specific) shapes is a side effect of our brain being hard wired to seek out patterns. More than seeking patterns, humans have built society upon patterns: patterns of speech, patterns of behavior, even patterns of history. It is only sensible, then, that we encourage the use of design patterns in coding.

## Once is a Mistake

Every programmer has the option to utilize their own stylistic choices when writing code. For small enough teams or projects, it might even be acceptable. However, once a team needs to collaborate or if a program is expected to be passed along to another employee the process can be made significantly easier by implementing patterns. To some degree, very nearly anyone writing code is using design patterns by default: what is a function except a fixed call with clearly defined inputs and clearly defined outputs?

Industry best practices are a great example: you *could* use GOTO, but it makes understanding your code more difficult, complexity problematic, and debugging a nightmare. By agreeing to how function names are derived, what order to put filetypes, etc. a group of people can quickly come up to speed on a completely novel problem. Faster understanding means faster solutions and less hair pulling.

## Twice is a Choice

Patterns help at smaller scales as well. When facing three exams in a row that will all use the same language and have similar requirements, there are two options. Door one is to enter each exam with a clean slate and build an entire webpage from scratch. Door two is to have a rough outline pre-drafted, with commonly used features readily available for copying and pasting and easily seen customization text. One of those options is objectively superior.

Patterns are used in all industries, from manufacturing clothing to stamping out steel parts for cars. They are make learning easier, finding breaks in the patterns simpler, and play into our existing biological hardware. Design pattern can save effort, time, and money: what more could be asked of a simple choice in behavior?
